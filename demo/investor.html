The signature reframe

Every incumbent sells the policyholder a sunk cost — a premium that vanishes if no claim is filed. Self-Insure turns that same premium into a recoverable investment in the home: a long-duration product whose float we invest, whose reserve we manage in-house, and whose return-of-premium promise self-selects the lowest-claim households into our pool.

Lower catastrophe frequency means our early book is exposed to attritional, model-able losses — not the tail events that punish coastal and wildfire states.

The anti-selection upside

Anti-selection, working in our favour

Conventional return-of-premium worries about adverse selection. Here the mechanism inverts: a policyholder who files a claim forfeits the recovery schedule, so the product attracts and rewards the households least likely to claim. The buyer's incentive and the carrier's loss ratio point the same direction — a structurally lower-claim pool that compounds the AI cost advantage.

Why the ladder is durable

The recovery is contingent on staying claims-free, so the schedule is funded by the very households least likely to draw on it — and any claim resets the policyholder's position. The long duration (10 / 20 / 30 years) gives the float engine a matched, predictable obligation to invest against. It is a savings product wrapped around protection.

What it means for retention

A policyholder approaching a 50%, 75%, or 100% milestone has a direct financial reason not to shop the policy. Return-of-premium converts price-shopping into tenure — lowering acquisition cost over the life of the book and reinforcing the anti-selection effect.

VI

The float engine — premium invested at ~4%, duration-matched to a 30-year obligation.

The return-of-premium product is a long-duration liability, which makes it an ideal float instrument. We invest the premium float at roughly 4%, duration-matched to the 30-year ROP obligation — and 30-year Treasuries are lockable at 4.72% today, a ~20bps structural edge. Float income is ~15–20% of revenue at scale (Self-Insure proforma v03). The Berkshire principle, applied to homeowners.

The Buffett analogy, made literal

Berkshire's edge was never just underwriting — it was the float: premiums held and invested between collection and payout. Self-Insure's ROP product extends that hold period to 30 years by design. We collect premium today, owe a defined recovery decades out, and invest the difference against a matched-duration book of high-grade fixed income.

Because the obligation's duration is known and long, we can lock 4.72% on 30-year Treasuries now while modelling float at a conservative ~4% — a ~20bps structural cushion built into the assumption.

VIII

The ROP reserve moat — a long-duration liability with no NAIC precedent, and a two-track path through it.

A 30-year return-of-premium obligation is a long-duration liability with no established NAIC reserving precedent. That is exactly what blocks naive entrants — and exactly the regulatory problem Self-Insure is built to solve. The plan is a deliberate two-track path that gets to market fast, then brings reserving in-house to capture the margin.

The two-track regulatory path
Years 1–3 · Option 3

100% cession to a life reinsurer

Ceding the ROP obligation to a life reinsurer secures fast approval — roughly 5–9 months to a licensed launch — and removes the reserving question from the critical path in the early years.

By Year 3 · Option 1

CRVM-analog reserve, in-house

Transitioning to an in-house CRVM-analog reserve once scaled captures the margin that ceding gives away — saving roughly $47M versus staying ceded across the plan.

The trust always exceeds the obligation

The reserving structure is conservative by design: the supporting trust is sized to stay ahead of the ROP liability throughout. At Year 10, for example, the trust holds roughly $210M against a ~$101M obligation — a 2.1× coverage ratio. The promise to return premium is over-collateralized, not aspirational.
